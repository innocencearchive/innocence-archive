# Book Research: "Grace and Justice on Death Row"

## Basic Book Information
- **Title**: Grace and Justice on Death Row: The Race against Time and Texas to Free an Innocent Man
- **Author(s)**: Brian W. Stolarz
- **Publication Year**: 2016 (October 25)
- **Publisher**: Skyhorse Publishing
- **Format**: Hardcover, 228 pages
- **Focus/Theme**: Wrongful conviction, death penalty, prosecutorial misconduct, attorney-client relationship
- **Key Statistics**: Alfred Dewayne Brown spent over twelve years in prison, including ten years on Texas' death row, for a crime he did not commit

## Wrongfully Convicted Individual Featured

### Alfred Dewayne Brown
- **Case Location**: Houston, Texas
- **Conviction Year**: 2005 (October 8)
- **Exoneration Year**: 2015 (released June 8); declared "actually innocent" in 2019
- **Crime Convicted Of**: Capital murder of Houston police officer Charles Clark and store clerk Alfredia Jones during a 2003 robbery
- **Sentence**: Death
- **Time Served**: Approximately 12 years (10 on death row)
- **Contributing Factors**: 
  - Mistaken witness identification
  - Perjury/false accusation (co-defendant testimony in exchange for plea deal)
  - Official misconduct (prosecutors withheld exculpatory evidence)
  - False testimony from girlfriend under duress
- **Legal Status**: Exonerated, declared "actually innocent" in 2019
- **Organizations Involved**: 
  - Brian Stolarz and his legal team
  - Texas Court of Criminal Appeals
  - Harris County District Attorney's Office (later acknowledged his innocence)
- **Cross-Reference**: Listed in the National Registry of Exonerations

## Book Research Notes

### Case Details
On April 3, 2003, three men robbed Ace America's Cash Express, a check-cashing store in Houston, Texas. During the robbery, store clerk Alfredia Jones (27) and responding Houston police officer Charles Clark (45) were fatally shot. The day after the crime, police arrested 21-year-old Alfred Brown, 21-year-old Dashan Glaspie, and 23-year-old Elijah Joubert on charges of capital murder.

Glaspie accepted a plea deal, receiving a 30-year sentence in exchange for testifying against Brown and Joubert. Both Brown and Joubert were convicted in separate trials and sentenced to death. Brown maintained his innocence throughout, claiming he was at his girlfriend Ericka Dockery's apartment at the time of the murders and had called her workplace from her landline.

The prosecution's case relied heavily on Glaspie's testimony and the testimony of Brown's girlfriend, Ericka Dockery, who initially corroborated Brown's alibi but changed her story after intense pressure from prosecutors and grand jurors. She was threatened with perjury charges, prison time, and losing custody of her children if she didn't change her testimony.

The critical piece of evidence supporting Brown's alibi—phone records showing he called Dockery's workplace from her apartment at the time of the crime—was withheld by prosecutor Dan Rizzo. These records were discovered years later in 2013 when a homicide detective found them while cleaning his garage. The records confirmed Brown's alibi that he had called Dockery's workplace at 10:08 a.m. on the morning of the murders, just as he had claimed from the beginning.

In 2014, the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals vacated Brown's conviction and ordered a new trial. On June 8, 2015, the Harris County District Attorney's Office dismissed all charges against Brown, citing insufficient evidence to retry the case. He was released after spending nearly 10 years on death row.

In 2019, Harris County District Attorney Kim Ogg officially declared Brown "actually innocent" after a special prosecutor's investigation. In 2020, the Texas Supreme Court ruled that Brown must be compensated for his wrongful imprisonment, potentially up to $2 million.

### Systemic Issues Identified
1. Prosecutorial misconduct and withholding of exculpatory evidence
2. Coercion of witnesses by prosecutors and grand jurors
3. Inadequate defense counsel during initial trials
4. Overreliance on testimony from co-defendants who receive plea deals
5. Racial bias in the criminal justice system
6. Structural barriers to proving innocence after conviction
7. The danger of executing innocent people in capital cases
8. The lengthy and difficult process of exoneration even when evidence of innocence exists

### Policy Recommendations
The book addresses many issues facing the criminal justice system and the death penalty, including:

1. Stronger enforcement of Brady disclosure requirements for prosecutors
2. Reforms to grand jury procedures to prevent intimidation of witnesses
3. Improved funding and resources for public defenders
4. Greater accountability for prosecutors who engage in misconduct
5. Reforms to compensation systems for exonerees
6. Reconsideration of the death penalty given the risk of executing innocent people

## Research Notes
The book not only details the legal battle to free an innocent man from death row but also explores how the attorney-client relationship between Stolarz and Brown evolved into a deep brotherhood. It provides a powerful firsthand account of the flaws in the criminal justice system while also offering a story of hope, perseverance, and redemption.

The foreword is written by Sister Helen Prejean, author of "Dead Man Walking," adding context about the broader issues of capital punishment and wrongful convictions. The book was a Washington Post bestseller and has been used to advocate for reforms in the criminal justice system.

Brian Stolarz, the author, is a lawyer who dedicated his career to securing Brown's freedom. His personal involvement in the case provides an intimate perspective on both the legal challenges and the human impact of wrongful conviction.
