## Framed: The Corruption and Cover-up Behind the Wrongful Conviction of William Michael Dillon

### Basic Book Information
- **Title**: Framed: The Corruption and Cover-up Behind the Wrongful Conviction of William Michael Dillon and his Twenty-Seven Year Fight for Freedom
- **Author(s)**: William Dillon and Ellen Moscovitz
- **Publication Year**: 2023
- **Publisher**: Unknown (possibly self-published)
- **Format**: Paperback, 370 pages
- **Focus/Theme**: Case study of William Michael Dillon's wrongful conviction, the corruption that led to it, and his 27-year fight for freedom
- **Key Statistics**: Dillon spent 27 years in prison for a crime he did not commit, at the time of his release in 2008, this was the longest time served by any DNA exoneree nationwide

### Wrongfully Convicted Individual Featured
- **Name**: William Michael Dillon (also known as Bill Dillon)
- **Case Location**: Brevard County, Florida
- **Conviction Year**: 1981
- **Exoneration Year**: 2008
- **Crime Convicted Of**: First-degree felony murder of James Dvorak
- **Sentence**: Life imprisonment
- **Time Served**: 27 years and 8 months
- **Contributing Factors**: 
  - Eyewitness misidentification
  - Informant/snitch testimony (cellmate who falsely claimed Dillon confessed)
  - Unreliable/limited forensic science (fraudulent dog scent evidence)
  - Perjured witness testimony
  - Manufactured evidence
  - Police and prosecutorial misconduct
- **Legal Status**: Exonerated through DNA testing, conviction vacated on November 14, 2008, released on November 18, 2008
- **Organizations Involved**: Innocence Project of Florida, Florida courts
- **Cross-Reference**: His case is documented in multiple wrongful conviction databases
- **Additional Notes**: Received $1.35 million in compensation from the Florida Legislature in 2012

### Case Details
On August 17, 1981, James Dvorak was found brutally beaten to death at Canova Beach in Florida. Several days after the murder, Dillon was at the beach and was approached by police who were questioning people about the crime. When Dillon mentioned that he had read about the murder in the newspaper and had seen the yellow police tape in the wooded area near the beach, police inferred that he had further information about the crime and brought him in for questioning.

The case against Dillon was built on four key pieces of flawed evidence:

1. **Fraudulent Dog Scent Evidence**: John Preston, a purported expert in scent tracking hired by the State, testified that his dog, Harass II, had linked Dillon to the crime scene and a yellow t-shirt believed to be worn by the killer. Preston was later thoroughly discredited as a fraud, with the Arizona Supreme Court calling him a "charlatan."

2. **False Testimony from a Jailhouse Informant**: Roger Dale Chapman, Dillon's cellmate, claimed that Dillon had confessed to the murder. After agreeing to testify against Dillon, the State dropped pending charges against Chapman for the rape of a sixteen-year-old girl. His testimony included numerous details that contradicted the facts of the case.

3. **Perjured Eyewitness Testimony**: Donna Parrish, Dillon's sometime sexual partner, was the only witness to testify that she saw Dillon at the crime scene. Less than a month after the trial, she recanted her testimony in its entirety, stating that she had been pressured by the Sheriff's Office and the State Attorney's Office and threatened with 25 years in prison. It was later revealed that she had a sexual relationship with the lead investigator.

4. **Unreliable Identification**: John Parker, who was legally blind in one eye, testified that he had picked up a hitchhiker near the crime scene who left a bloody yellow t-shirt in his truck. At trial, he identified this man as Dillon, despite the fact that his description of the assailant differed markedly from Dillon's actual characteristics.

The yellow t-shirt was a key piece of evidence, with the State repeatedly linking it to both the crime scene and to Dillon. However, DNA testing in 2007 revealed that DNA from sweat on the bloody t-shirt did not come from Dillon, proving his innocence.

### Book Research Notes
- **Key Quotes**: "Thank God for DNA" - William Dillon, expressing gratitude for the technology that eventually freed him
- **Systemic Issues Identified**: 
  - Use of fraudulent forensic evidence (dog scent tracking)
  - Reliance on jailhouse informants who receive benefits for testimony
  - Witness intimidation and coercion by law enforcement
  - Lack of accountability for prosecutors and police who engage in misconduct
  - Inadequate compensation and support for exonerees
- **Policy Recommendations**: 
  - Reform of forensic science standards and practices
  - Limitations on the use of jailhouse informant testimony
  - Greater accountability for prosecutorial and police misconduct
  - Improved compensation and support for exonerees
- **Additional Resources**: 
  - William Dillon released a music album titled "Black Robes and Lawyers" about his experience
  - Florida Innocence Project website contains detailed information about his case
  - Dillon has become an advocate for exonerees who are released from prison with little support

### Research Notes
This book is particularly significant as it tells the story of one of the longest-serving DNA exonerees in U.S. history. Dillon's case highlights multiple systemic issues in the criminal justice system, including the use of fraudulent forensic evidence, witness intimidation, and the challenges faced by exonerees after release.

After his release, Dillon had to wait four years until 2012 to receive compensation through a claims bill passed by the Florida Legislature, which awarded him $1.35 million ($50,000 for each year of wrongful imprisonment). He has since become an advocate for exonerees and has pursued a career in music, using it as a way to tell his story of injustice and eventual freedom.

The book appears to be a recent publication (2023) and has been recognized by the Innocence Project as one of the best books of 2024, suggesting it provides valuable insights into wrongful convictions and the experiences of exonerees.
