### Case Details
Cecil Johnson was convicted in 1981 of three counts of first-degree murder for the deaths of Bobby Bell Jr., James Moore, and Charles House during an apparent robbery at Bob Bell's Market in Nashville, Tennessee, on July 5, 1980. He was 24 years old at the time of his conviction.

Johnson was arrested on July 6, 1980, based on information provided to police by Bell immediately after the robberies and murders. He was subsequently convicted and sentenced to death. His convictions and sentence were upheld by the Tennessee Supreme Court on May 3, 1982.

According to available information, Johnson was convicted despite there being no physical evidence, no murder weapon, and no robbery proceeds recovered. His case involved eyewitness identification, which studies have shown to be a leading cause of confirmed wrongful convictions.

Johnson spent 29 years on death row before being executed on December 2, 2009. The book does not primarily focus on claims of innocence or wrongful conviction but rather on the relationship that developed between Johnson and the author's family.

### Research Notes
"He Called Me Sister: A True Story of Finding Humanity on Death Row" is not primarily focused on wrongful conviction or claims of innocence, but rather on the personal relationship that developed between the author and Cecil Johnson, a man on death row. The book offers a unique perspective on death row by focusing on the human connections that can form even in such difficult circumstances.

The story begins when the author's husband, Alan Robertson, signed up through a church program to visit Cecil Johnson on death row, offering friendship and compassion. Initially, Suzanne had no intention of being involved, but gradually, through phone calls and letters, she began to empathize with and understand Johnson. Over time, they developed a close friendship that transcended differences of race, privilege, and circumstance.

The book recounts their fifteen-year friendship and how trust and compassion were forged despite the difficult circumstances. Interestingly, the author notes that Cecil ended up ministering more to her family than they did to him, highlighting his capacity for care and compassion despite his situation.

While the book does not appear to make explicit claims about Johnson's innocence or argue that he was wrongfully convicted, it does mention that his conviction was based on eyewitness testimony without physical evidence, murder weapon, or recovered robbery proceeds. Eyewitness misidentification is known to be a leading cause of wrongful convictions, which raises questions about the reliability of the evidence in his case.

The book serves as a reminder of the humanity of people on death row and challenges readers to look beyond labels and circumstances to see the person beneath. It also implicitly raises questions about the death penalty system and its impact on all involved, including the families of those executed and the families of victims.
