# Book Research: "The Wrong Carlos"

- **Author(s)**: James S. Liebman and the Columbia DeLuna Project

- **Publisher**: Columbia University Press
- **Format**: Hardcover and Paperback, 448 pages
- **ISBN**: 9780231167222 (hardcover), 9780231167239 (paperback)

## Wrongfully Convicted Individual Featured

### Carlos DeLuna
- **Case Location**: Corpus Christi, Texas
- **Conviction Year**: 1983
- **Execution Year**: 1989
- **Crime Convicted Of**: Murder of Wanda Lopez, a convenience store clerk
- **Sentence**: Death
- **Time Served**: 6 years on death row
- **Contributing Factors**: 
  - Single, nighttime, cross-ethnic eyewitness identification
  - No corroborating forensic evidence
  - Inadequate legal representation
  - Prosecutorial misconduct (suppression of evidence)
  - Police failure to investigate alternative suspects
  - Mistaken identity with another man named Carlos Hernandez
- **Legal Status**: Executed despite significant evidence of innocence uncovered later
- **Organizations Involved**: 
  - Columbia Law School
  - Columbia Human Rights Law Review
- **Cross-Reference**: Case was the subject of a documentary film "The Phantom" (2021)

## Book Research Notes

### Case Details
On February 4, 1983, Wanda Lopez was stabbed to death while working at a Sigmor Shamrock gas station convenience store in Corpus Christi, Texas. The killer used a lock-blade buck knife. Lopez had called 911 before the attack, reporting a suspicious man with a knife in the store. The police arrived shortly after the murder and began a manhunt in the area.

About 40 minutes after the crime, police found Carlos DeLuna hiding under a pickup truck several blocks from the crime scene. He was arrested, and a single eyewitness, Kevan Baker, identified him as the man he had seen coming out of the store. This cross-ethnic, nighttime identification became the cornerstone of the prosecution's case against DeLuna. No physical evidence connected DeLuna to the crime—no blood was found on his clothing despite the bloody nature of the crime, and no fingerprints matched him.

From the beginning, DeLuna maintained his innocence and claimed that another man named Carlos—Carlos Hernandez—had committed the murder. The prosecution dismissed this claim, with the lead prosecutor telling the jury that Carlos Hernandez was a "phantom" of DeLuna's imagination. DeLuna was convicted and sentenced to death in 1983.

The Columbia DeLuna Project, led by Professor James Liebman, conducted a comprehensive six-year investigation into the case. They discovered that not only did Carlos Hernandez exist, but he was well-known to local law enforcement as someone with a history of knife crimes similar to the Lopez murder. Hernandez had been arrested for murdering another woman with a lock-blade buck knife, and he stabbed and attempted to rape another woman while DeLuna was on death row.

The investigation uncovered a police audiotape—suppressed during DeLuna's trial—showing that police had chased another man who matched Hernandez's description (but not DeLuna's) for 30 minutes immediately following the crime. The team also found that Hernandez had spent years bragging around Corpus Christi that he, not his "tocayo" (namesake) Carlos DeLuna, had committed the murder.

The two men looked remarkably similar, to the point where families of both men mistook photos of them for each other. DeLuna had childlike intelligence and no history of violent crime, while Hernandez had a long record of violent offenses involving knives.

Despite mounting evidence of his innocence, Carlos DeLuna was executed by lethal injection on December 7, 1989.

### Systemic Issues Identified
1. Unreliability of eyewitness identification, particularly cross-ethnic identifications
2. Inadequate police investigation procedures
3. Prosecutorial misconduct, including suppression of exculpatory evidence
4. Inadequate legal representation for indigent defendants
5. Failure of courts to properly review evidence of innocence
6. Racial and socioeconomic bias in the criminal justice system
7. Finality of the death penalty, which prevents correction of wrongful convictions
8. Lack of accountability for prosecutors and police who contribute to wrongful convictions
9. Systemic pressures to solve high-profile cases quickly
10. Failure to properly preserve and test forensic evidence

### Policy Recommendations
1. Reform of eyewitness identification procedures
2. Increased funding for public defenders and court-appointed attorneys
3. Enhanced disclosure requirements for prosecutors
4. Videotaping of police investigations and interrogations
5. Improved preservation of crime scene evidence
6. More rigorous standards for admissibility of forensic evidence
7. Enhanced review procedures for capital cases
8. Reforms to allow for consideration of new evidence of innocence
9. Accountability measures for prosecutors who engage in misconduct
10. Abolition or moratorium on the death penalty

## Research Notes
"The Wrong Carlos: Anatomy of a Wrongful Execution" represents one of the most comprehensive investigations into a potential wrongful execution ever conducted. The book is the result of a six-year investigation by Columbia Law School professor James Liebman and his students, who meticulously documented the case of Carlos DeLuna.

The investigation compiled an extraordinary collection of primary documents and evidence, including complete police and court files, enhanced crime scene photos that revealed evidence never recognized by police (including bloody footprints), a police audiotape of the manhunt, full criminal records of key actors, television news clips, newspaper articles, and notes from hundreds of witness interviews.

What makes this book particularly significant is that it documents a case where an execution has already occurred, making it impossible to exonerate the defendant through traditional legal means. Instead, the authors present the evidence they've gathered and invite readers to come to their own conclusions about DeLuna's guilt or innocence.

The book serves as both a detailed case study of a specific wrongful execution and a broader critique of the death penalty system in the United States. It highlights how common factors in wrongful convictions—faulty eyewitness testimony, inadequate legal representation, and prosecutorial misconduct—can lead to irreversible injustice when the death penalty is involved.

The case of Carlos DeLuna has become one of the most compelling examples used by death penalty opponents to illustrate the risk of executing innocent people. The thoroughness of the investigation and the strength of the evidence suggesting DeLuna's innocence make it difficult to dismiss as an anomaly or an acceptable risk in the administration of capital punishment.
