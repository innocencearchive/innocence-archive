- **Additional Notes**: The men were from Virginia but were wrongfully convicted in Louisiana for the murder of an elderly couple, William and Callie Frost

### Case Details
The book explores Virginia's history with capital punishment, starting with the first execution in 1608 through its abolition on July 1, 2021. It details how Virginia's death penalty system was deeply rooted in racism and shrouded by secrecy, characteristic of states whose criminal laws grew out of a legacy of slavery, lynching, and Jim Crow segregation.

Virginia led the nation with 1,390 executions throughout its history and executed more enslaved individuals and women than any other state. In the modern era (post-1976), Virginia executed 73% of those receiving death sentences, compared to the nationwide average of 16%. The commonwealth's curtailment of the death row appeals process in 1999 reduced the timeframe for appeals to less than 5 years, half the national average at the time.

The book catalogs the 20-year effort by Virginians for Alternatives to the Death Penalty (VADP) to abolish the death penalty, including their strategies, challenges, and eventual success. A key part of their strategy was garnering support from conservatives by appealing to arguments about government overreach and fiscal responsibility.

### Research Notes
"Closing the Slaughterhouse: The Inside Story of Death Penalty Abolition in Virginia" by Dale M. Brumfield provides a comprehensive account of Virginia's journey from being the nation's leading executioner to becoming the first Southern state to abolish the death penalty in 2021. The book explores the historical context of capital punishment in Virginia, the efforts of abolition advocates, and the systemic issues that ultimately led to the death penalty's demise in the commonwealth.

The book highlights how Virginia's death penalty system was deeply rooted in racism, with the state executing more enslaved individuals and women than any other state throughout its history. In the modern era (post-1976), Virginia was particularly aggressive in carrying out executions, with 73% of death sentences resulting in execution compared to the national average of 16%. The commonwealth also implemented procedural rules designed to expedite executions by limiting appeals.

While the book primarily focuses on the abolition movement rather than specific wrongful conviction cases, it does address the risk of executing innocent people as one of the arguments against the death penalty. The book mentions the case of Michael Graham and Albert Burrell, two men from Roanoke, Virginia who were wrongfully convicted and spent 14 years on Louisiana's death row before being exonerated. Their case exemplifies the flaws in the death penalty system, including the use of unreliable witnesses, prosecutorial misconduct, and the lack of physical evidence.

The book details the 20-year effort by Virginians for Alternatives to the Death Penalty (VADP) to abolish capital punishment in the state. Their strategy evolved over time, particularly after 2015 when they adopted a 5-year plan for abolition that included garnering support from conservatives by appealing to arguments about government overreach and fiscal responsibility. The organization also highlighted racial disparities, police and prosecutorial misconduct, and the risk of executing innocent people as reasons to end the death penalty.

The book culminates with Governor Ralph Northam signing death penalty abolition into law on March 24, 2021, "inside a tent in the shadow of the state's execution chamber at Greensville Correctional Center." This symbolic location emphasized the historic nature of Virginia becoming the first former Confederate state to abolish the death penalty.

Published by Abolition Press on May 6, 2022, "Closing the Slaughterhouse" serves as both a historical account and a case study in successful advocacy for criminal justice reform. The book's detailed examination of how Virginia moved from leading executioner to groundbreaking abolitionist state provides valuable insights for those studying wrongful convictions and the movement to end capital punishment in the United States.
