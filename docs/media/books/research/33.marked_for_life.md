### Case Details
On July 25, 1989, police arrested 11 people on charges of operating a $20 million-a-year cocaine distribution network in Somerset and Middlesex Counties in New Jersey. Somerset County Prosecutor Nicholas Bissell Jr. announced the arrests and declared that the drug ring was overseen by 28-year-old Isaac Wright Jr., who was a music producer and had co-founded a singing group called The Cover Girls, which included his wife, Sunshine Wright.

By the time Wright came to trial, eight of the defendants had pled guilty and agreed to cooperate with the prosecution, and charges against two others had been dismissed. The prosecution's key witnesses included Roberto Alexander, who testified that he sold Wright cocaine on the day of the arrest. Alexander had pled guilty and was facing a 15-year prison term. During cross-examination by Wright, who represented himself at trial, Alexander admitted that a major reason he had pled guilty was that the state agreed to drop all charges against his wife and brother.

Other co-defendants also testified against Wright, claiming they worked for him and brought him up to $70,000 a week in drug profits. However, one witness, Willie Sirmans, recanted his testimony implicating Wright during the trial, stating he had agreed to plead guilty and cooperate because he wanted to avoid a long prison term. Sirmans testified he would "say anything" to avoid going to prison.

On April 23, 1991, Wright testified and denied any involvement in the sale of cocaine. He pointed out that if he were making tens of thousands of dollars weekly as alleged, he wouldn't have been living in a modest condominium, driving old cars, and having only a few hundred dollars in his bank account. Despite his defense, on April 26, 1991, the jury convicted Wright of 10 charges of drug possession and sale, including a charge of being a leader of a narcotics network. In May 1991, he was sentenced to life in prison with no possibility of parole for at least 30 years.

While in prison, Wright focused on doing legal work for other prisoners as well as working on his own case. He developed a legal argument challenging the jury instructions in the drug kingpin charge. In 1995, the New Jersey Appellate Division vacated Wright's conviction on the drug kingpin charge, agreeing that the jury had not been correctly instructed. The court affirmed his convictions on the other nine charges.

The prosecution appealed, but in May 1996, the New Jersey Supreme Court upheld the appellate court's decision. By that time, the trial judge, Michael Imbriani, was no longer on the bench, having pled guilty to stealing from business partners. Prosecutor Bissell had also been convicted on federal tax and fraud charges and had committed suicide in November 1996 while fleeing from authorities.

On December 18, 1996, Wright was released on bail. On December 17, 1998, after a series of hearings during which two prosecution witnesses recanted their trial testimony and admitted they had falsely implicated Wright, Superior Court Judge Michael Imbriani vacated all of Wright's convictions. The Somerset County Prosecutor's Office dismissed all charges against Wright.

### Research Notes
"Marked for Life: One Man's Fight for Justice From the Inside" is a powerful memoir by Isaac Wright Jr. with Jon Sternfeld that chronicles Wright's extraordinary journey from being wrongfully convicted as a drug kingpin to becoming a lawyer who fights for justice for others. Published by St. Martin's Press on November 8, 2022, the 336-page hardcover book serves as the basis for the ABC television show "For Life."

In 1989, Wright was arrested and accused of being the mastermind behind a $20 million-a-year cocaine distribution network in New Jersey. Despite having no physical evidence linking him to drug trafficking and maintaining his innocence throughout, Wright was convicted in 1991 largely based on testimony from co-defendants who had made deals with prosecutors. He was sentenced to life in prison with no possibility of parole for at least 30 years under New Jersey's drug kingpin statute.

What makes Wright's story particularly remarkable is that while incarcerated, he educated himself in law and began working as a prison paralegal, helping other inmates with their cases while simultaneously fighting his own conviction. Wright represented himself at trial and continued his legal battle from behind bars, eventually uncovering prosecutorial misconduct by Somerset County Prosecutor Nicholas Bissell Jr., who was later convicted on federal charges and committed suicide while fleeing authorities.

In 1995, Wright's legal arguments led the New Jersey Appellate Division to vacate his conviction on the drug kingpin charge, though his other convictions remained. He was released on bail in 1996, and after further hearings where prosecution witnesses recanted their testimony and admitted to falsely implicating Wright, all charges against him were dismissed in 1998.

Following his exoneration, Wright pursued a formal legal education, earned his law degree, and became a licensed attorney. In a poetic turn of justice, he returned to practice law in the same courtroom where he had been wrongfully convicted years earlier.

The book explores not only the details of Wright's case but also the broader systemic issues in the criminal justice system, including prosecutorial misconduct, racial discrimination, and the challenges faced by those who maintain their innocence. Reviews describe the memoir as "powerful," "thought-provoking," and "a surprisingly positive memoir written by a man who has every reason to be angry."

"Marked for Life" stands as both a personal testament to resilience and determination and an indictment of the flaws in the American criminal justice system that can lead to wrongful convictions. Wright's story illustrates how factors such as perjury, false accusations, and official misconduct can result in innocent people being imprisoned, while also demonstrating the possibility of fighting for justice even from within the system that has failed them.
