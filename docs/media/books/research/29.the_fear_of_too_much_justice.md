### Research Notes
"The Fear of Too Much Justice: Race, Poverty, and the Persistence of Inequality in the Criminal Courts" is a comprehensive examination of the failures of the U.S. criminal justice system, particularly as they affect poor defendants and people of color. The title references a quote from Supreme Court Justice Harry Blackmun, who in a death penalty case wrote about "a fear of too much justice" when the Court refused to address racial disparities in capital sentencing.

The book is co-authored by Stephen B. Bright, a renowned death penalty attorney who served as the director of the Southern Center for Human Rights and teaches law at Georgetown and Yale Universities, and James Kwak, a legal scholar who serves as vice chair of the Board of the Southern Center for Human Rights and has authored several books on economics and public policy.

According to available sources, the book offers a "heart-wrenching overview of how the criminal legal system fails to live up to the values of equality and justice." It examines various aspects of the criminal justice system, from wrongful convictions and death penalty cases to the exploitation of poor defendants by private probation companies.

The book includes a foreword by Bryan Stevenson, founder of the Equal Justice Initiative and author of "Just Mercy," who describes it as "an urgently needed analysis of our collective failure to confront and overcome racial bias and bigotry, the abuse of power, and the multiple ways in which the death penalty's profound unfairness requires its abolition."

While the available sources do not detail specific wrongful conviction cases featured in the book, John Grisham's endorsement suggests that Bright's forty years of experience working "to protect the poor and innocent, and to expose the truth behind capital punishment, wrongful convictions, corrupt prosecutors, incompetent judges, and all the other bad actors" informs the book's content.

The book not only identifies problems but also highlights solutions, offering "examples from places around the country that are making progress toward justice." This suggests a forward-looking approach that goes beyond critique to offer potential paths for reform.

Publishers Weekly described the book as an "urgent call to action" and "an invaluable resource," indicating its potential impact on criminal justice reform efforts. The book's release in paperback format in April 2025 suggests ongoing relevance and demand for its insights.
