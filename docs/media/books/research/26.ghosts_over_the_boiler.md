# Book Research: Wrongful Conviction Media Sources

## Ghosts Over the Boiler: Voices from Alabama's Death Row

### Basic Book Information
- **Title**: Ghosts Over the Boiler: Voices from Alabama's Death Row
- **Author(s)**: Project Hope to Abolish the Death Penalty (edited by Katie Owens-Murphy)
- **Publication Year**: 2023 (March 15)
- **Publisher**: Vanderbilt University Press
- **Format**: Paperback, 344 pages
- **Focus/Theme**: A collection of writings from condemned men on Alabama's death row spanning 30 years (1990-2020), providing insight into their humanity, experiences, and perspectives
- **Key Statistics**: The book mentions that 8 men have been exonerated from Alabama's death row after being wrongfully convicted

### Wrongfully Convicted Individual Featured
- **Name**: Gary Drinkard
- **Case Location**: Alabama
- **Conviction Year**: 1995
- **Exoneration Year**: 2001
- **Crime Convicted Of**: Murder of a 65-year-old automotive junk dealer in Decatur, Alabama
- **Sentence**: Death penalty
- **Time Served**: Nearly 6 years on death row
- **Contributing Factors**: 
  - False accusation
  - Inadequate legal defense (assigned two lawyers with no experience trying criminal cases)
  - Prosecutorial misconduct
  - Testimony from incentivized witnesses (his half-sister and her common-law husband, who faced charges that would be dismissed in exchange for their testimony)
  - Failure to present alibi evidence (attorneys didn't bring up that a dog breeder and her boyfriend had been with Gary at his home the night of the murders)
- **Legal Status**: Exonerated
- **Organizations Involved**: Equal Justice Initiative, Southern Center for Human Rights, Witness to Innocence
- **Cross-Reference**: His case has been documented by Witness to Innocence and other wrongful conviction advocacy organizations
- **Additional Notes**: After exoneration, Drinkard became an advocate against the death penalty and continues the work of Project Hope as a free person; he works as a Peer Specialist for Witness to Innocence, supporting fellow exonerated death row survivors

### Case Details
The book is unique in that it doesn't focus on a single case but rather presents a collection of writings from multiple men on Alabama's death row over a 30-year period. These writings provide insight into their experiences, perspectives, and humanity.

Gary Drinkard's case is one of the wrongful conviction stories featured in the book. Drinkard was convicted of the robbery and murder of a 65-year-old automotive junk dealer in Decatur, Alabama. Despite having a debilitating back injury that limited his physical abilities and having alibi witnesses who could confirm he was home at the time of the murder, he was convicted and sentenced to death in 1995.

The case against him rested primarily on testimony from his half-sister and her common-law husband, both of whom were facing charges for unrelated crimes that would be dismissed in exchange for their testimony against Drinkard. His court-appointed attorneys had no experience with criminal cases and failed to present evidence that could have proven his innocence, such as testimony from a dog breeder and her boyfriend who had visited Drinkard at his home on the night of the murders.

In 2000, the Alabama Supreme Court ordered a new trial because of prosecutorial misconduct. With the help of the Southern Center for Human Rights, Drinkard won an acquittal in 2001 and was released after spending nearly six years on death row.

### Research Notes
"Ghosts Over the Boiler: Voices from Alabama's Death Row" is unique among books about wrongful convictions as it provides a platform for the voices of the men themselves, rather than focusing solely on legal analysis or journalistic accounts. The book is organized into five chronological parts spanning the 30-year history of Project Hope and includes writings that have appeared in their quarterly newsletter, "On the Wings of Hope."

Project Hope to Abolish the Death Penalty is particularly notable as it is a prisoner-founded and run organization established in 1989. It is run by death-sentenced inmates who solicit contributions from other death-sentenced people in Alabama, author works, and produce their quarterly newsletter.

The book deliberately avoids focusing on the crimes for which the men have been convicted, instead emphasizing their humanity, creativity, and capacity for growth and redemption. This approach challenges readers to see beyond the singular view of people on death row as defined solely by their alleged crimes.

The inclusion of Gary Drinkard's story of wrongful conviction and exoneration adds an important dimension to the book, highlighting the risk of executing innocent people. As the 93rd death row exoneree in the United States, Drinkard's experience underscores the fallibility of the criminal justice system and the irreversible nature of capital punishment.

The book serves as both a literary work and a powerful advocacy tool against the death penalty, preserving the voices and perspectives of men who have largely been silenced and forgotten by society.
