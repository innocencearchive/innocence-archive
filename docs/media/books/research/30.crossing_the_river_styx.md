### Case Details
While specific wrongful conviction cases are not detailed in the available sources, the book recounts Ford's experiences with numerous death row inmates in Virginia. Ford served as the head chaplain on Virginia's death row from 1984 until his retirement in 2001 (after suffering a severe brain injury). During this time, he ministered to all Virginia death row inmates and was present for 28 executions.

The book describes the inhumane treatment experienced by many prisoners and Ford's efforts to ensure humane conditions of confinement, despite resistance from some prison administrators. Ford points to the risk of executing innocent people and the persistence of botched executions as concerns about the death penalty system.

The book includes specific anecdotes about Ford's experiences, such as his interaction with Ricky Boggs during his execution in 1990, when Ford was nearly electrocuted himself while comforting Boggs in the electric chair during a delay.

### Research Notes
"Crossing the River Styx: The Memoir of a Death Row Chaplain" provides a unique perspective on the death penalty through the eyes of Russ Ford, who served as the head chaplain on Virginia's death row from 1984 to 2001. The book is co-authored by Ford along with Todd C. Peppers (a public affairs professor at Roanoke College) and Charles Peppers.

The memoir is based on notes and journals that Ford kept during his tenure as chaplain. He describes his experiences ministering to condemned prisoners, witnessing their final moments, and advocating for humane treatment within the prison system. Ford formed relationships with more than a dozen condemned prisoners and stood death watch with each man facing execution, praying with them and reading them their last rites.

While the book does not appear to focus specifically on wrongful convictions, Ford does express concern about the risk of executing innocent people as one of the problems with the death penalty system. This concern is mentioned alongside other issues such as the inhumane treatment of prisoners, insufficient medical assistance, poor management, and botched executions.

The book does not shy away from acknowledging the serious crimes committed by many death row inmates. Ford specifically mentions that "these were some terrible men" and singles out inmate Alton Waye as "a really bad guy." The authors make it a point to name the victims of the crimes committed by the inmates discussed in the book.

At the same time, the book highlights the humanity and spiritual journeys of the condemned. Ford describes how many inmates experienced profound and sincere spiritual conversions while waiting to die. One example mentioned is Michael Smith, who underwent an intense religious conversion before his execution in the electric chair in 1986.

The book also touches on the psychological toll that working on death row took on Ford himself. Today, 40 years after his experiences, he walks with a cane and suffers from post-traumatic stress disorder. This personal aspect adds another dimension to the discussion of the impact of the death penalty on all involved, not just the condemned and their victims.

Virginia abolished the death penalty in 2021, becoming the first former-Confederate state to do so. Ford is described as "circumspect" about the possibility of its return, noting the effective legal mechanisms that grew to oppose capital punishment and the enormous cost of executing someone.

This memoir provides valuable insights into the death penalty system from someone who witnessed it firsthand over many years. While it may not focus specifically on wrongful convictions, its discussion of the risk of executing innocent people contributes to the broader conversation about the flaws in the criminal justice system that can lead to such tragic outcomes.
