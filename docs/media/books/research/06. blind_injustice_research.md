# Book Research: Blind Injustice

## Basic Book Information
- **Title**: Blind Injustice: A Former Prosecutor Exposes the Psychology and Politics of Wrongful Convictions
- **Author**: Mark Godsey
- **Publication Year**: 2017
- **Publisher**: University of California Press
- **Format**: Hardcover, paperback, e-book
- **Focus/Theme**: Psychological factors contributing to wrongful convictions, cognitive biases in the justice system
- **Key Statistics**: Draws on the author's experience with the Ohio Innocence Project, which has helped free 43 wrongfully convicted individuals

## About the Author
- **Mark Godsey**: Former federal prosecutor turned innocence advocate
- **Background**: Professor of Law at the University of Cincinnati College of Law
- **Organization**: Co-founder and Director of the Ohio Innocence Project
- **Experience**: Transitioned from being a "prosecutor's prosecutor" who didn't believe innocent people were in prison to becoming a leading advocate for the wrongfully convicted

## Book Summary
"Blind Injustice" examines the psychological and institutional factors that contribute to wrongful convictions. Drawing on his unique perspective as both a former prosecutor and later an innocence advocate, Mark Godsey explores how cognitive biases, institutional pressures, and psychological phenomena lead well-intentioned professionals in the criminal justice system to convict innocent people.

The book identifies several psychological factors that contribute to wrongful convictions, including tunnel vision, confirmation bias, memory malleability, administrative evil, and dehumanization. Godsey argues that these psychological factors affect all human beings, including judges, police officers, prosecutors, and jurors, making the system vulnerable to error despite good intentions.

Through case studies from his work with the Ohio Innocence Project and his own experiences as a prosecutor, Godsey illustrates how these psychological factors play out in real cases. He also proposes reforms to address these issues and reduce the likelihood of wrongful convictions.

## Wrongfully Convicted Individuals Featured

### 1. Clarence Elkins
- **Case Location**: Ohio
- **Conviction Year**: 1999
- **Exoneration Year**: 2005
- **Crime Convicted Of**: Murder of his mother-in-law and sexual assault of his niece
- **Sentence**: Life imprisonment
- **Time Served**: 7 years
- **Contributing Factors**: Eyewitness misidentification, tunnel vision by investigators
- **Legal Status**: Exonerated through DNA evidence
- **Organizations Involved**: Ohio Innocence Project
- **Additional Notes**: 
  - Elkins collected DNA evidence from the actual perpetrator (another inmate) while in prison
  - Case demonstrates how tunnel vision can lead investigators to focus on the wrong suspect

### 2. Nancy Smith and Joseph Allen
- **Case Location**: Lorain, Ohio
- **Conviction Year**: 1994
- **Exoneration Year**: 2009 (though legal battles continued)
- **Crime Convicted Of**: Sexually abusing children on Smith's school bus
- **Sentence**: Long prison terms
- **Contributing Factors**: Suggestive questioning of children, memory malleability
- **Legal Status**: Convictions overturned
- **Organizations Involved**: Ohio Innocence Project
- **Additional Notes**: 
  - Case similar to other daycare panic cases of the era
  - Demonstrates how children's memories can be influenced by suggestive questioning

### 3. Dean Gillispie
- **Case Location**: Ohio
- **Conviction Year**: 1991
- **Exoneration Year**: 2011
- **Crime Convicted Of**: Rape and kidnapping
- **Sentence**: 22-56 years
- **Time Served**: 20 years
- **Contributing Factors**: Police misconduct, withheld evidence
- **Legal Status**: Exonerated
- **Organizations Involved**: Ohio Innocence Project
- **Additional Notes**: 
  - Police withheld exculpatory evidence that would have proven his innocence
  - Case demonstrates how tunnel vision can lead to misconduct

### 4. David Ayers
- **Case Location**: Cleveland, Ohio
- **Conviction Year**: 2000
- **Exoneration Year**: 2011
- **Crime Convicted Of**: Murder of elderly woman
- **Sentence**: Life imprisonment
- **Time Served**: 11 years
- **Contributing Factors**: Police misconduct, false testimony
- **Legal Status**: Exonerated through DNA evidence
- **Organizations Involved**: Ohio Innocence Project
- **Additional Notes**: 
  - Won a civil judgment against the city of Cleveland
  - Case demonstrates institutional resistance to acknowledging mistakes

## Systemic Issues Identified
1. Cognitive biases (tunnel vision, confirmation bias)
2. Memory malleability and eyewitness misidentification
3. Institutional pressures to obtain convictions
4. Dehumanization of defendants
5. Administrative evil (compartmentalization of responsibility)
6. Resistance to acknowledging mistakes
7. Lack of accountability for prosecutors and police
8. Psychological phenomena that affect all human decision-making

## Policy Recommendations
1. Increased awareness of cognitive biases among justice system professionals
2. Reforms to eyewitness identification procedures
3. Recording of all police interrogations
4. Open file discovery policies for prosecutors
5. Conviction integrity units in prosecutors' offices
6. Enhanced training for police, prosecutors, and judges on psychological factors
7. Cultural changes to allow acknowledgment of mistakes
8. Reforms to prevent tunnel vision in investigations

## Research Notes
- The book takes a psychological approach to understanding wrongful convictions rather than focusing solely on legal or procedural issues
- Godsey's perspective as both a former prosecutor and innocence advocate provides unique insights
- The book connects psychological research to real-world cases
- Many of the cases featured involve the Ohio Innocence Project, which Godsey directs
- The book emphasizes that wrongful convictions often result from good people making mistakes due to psychological factors rather than malicious intent
