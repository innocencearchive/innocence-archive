# Book Research: Wrongful Conviction Media Sources

## Worse Than Slavery: Parchman Farm And The Ordeal Of Jim Crow Justice

### Basic Book Information
- **Title**: Worse Than Slavery: Parchman Farm And The Ordeal Of Jim Crow Justice
- **Author(s)**: David M. Oshinsky
- **Publication Year**: 1996 (paperback edition released April 22, 1997)
- **Publisher**: Free Press
- **Format**: Hardcover, paperback
- **Focus/Theme**: The history of Parchman Farm penitentiary in Mississippi and how the criminal justice system was used to maintain racial control after slavery
- **Key Statistics**: 320 pages

### Wrongfully Convicted Individuals Featured
While the book primarily focuses on the broader historical context of the criminal justice system in the post-Civil War South rather than specific wrongful conviction cases, it provides essential background on the system that led to numerous wrongful convictions. The book has been referenced in discussions of several notable wrongful conviction cases from Mississippi, including:

1. **Levon Brooks**
   - **Case Location**: Noxubee County, Mississippi
   - **Conviction Year**: 1992
   - **Exoneration Year**: 2008
   - **Crime Convicted Of**: Capital murder of a 3-year-old girl
   - **Sentence**: Life in prison
   - **Time Served**: 16 years
   - **Contributing Factors**: Flawed forensic evidence, particularly discredited bite mark analysis by Dr. Michael West
   - **Legal Status**: Exonerated through DNA evidence that identified the actual perpetrator
   - **Organizations Involved**: The Innocence Project

2. **Kennedy Brewer**
   - **Case Location**: Noxubee County, Mississippi
   - **Conviction Year**: 1995
   - **Exoneration Year**: 2008
   - **Crime Convicted Of**: Capital murder of a 3-year-old girl
   - **Sentence**: Death row
   - **Time Served**: 13 years (7 on death row)
   - **Contributing Factors**: Flawed forensic evidence, particularly discredited bite mark analysis by Dr. Michael West
   - **Legal Status**: Exonerated through DNA evidence that identified the actual perpetrator (Justin Johnson, the same person responsible for the crime Brooks was wrongfully convicted of)
   - **Organizations Involved**: The Innocence Project

### Research Notes
"Worse Than Slavery" provides a comprehensive historical account of how the criminal justice system in the post-Civil War South, particularly in Mississippi, was designed to maintain racial control after the formal abolition of slavery. The book focuses on Parchman Farm, Mississippi's notorious state penitentiary, which was modeled after a slave plantation and became synonymous with cruelty and inhumanity.

Oshinsky meticulously documents how the criminal justice system was weaponized against Black Americans through Black Codes, convict leasing, and other mechanisms that effectively circumvented the 13th Amendment's prohibition of slavery "except as a punishment for crime." The book shows how minor offenses like vagrancy were used to arrest and incarcerate Black Americans, who were then leased out to private businesses or forced to work on state-run prison farms like Parchman.

While the book does not focus specifically on wrongful convictions in the modern sense, it provides essential context for understanding how the criminal justice system in states like Mississippi was fundamentally designed to control and exploit Black Americans rather than deliver justice. This historical foundation helps explain the environment in which more recent wrongful convictions, like those of Levon Brooks and Kennedy Brewer, occurred.

The book has been referenced in discussions of these and other wrongful conviction cases from Mississippi, highlighting the continuity between historical injustices and contemporary failures of the criminal justice system. The cases of Brooks and Brewer, both wrongfully convicted of similar crimes in Noxubee County, Mississippi, and both victims of flawed forensic evidence, exemplify how the legacy of racial bias in the criminal justice system continues to result in wrongful convictions.

Oshinsky's work is particularly valuable for understanding the historical roots of mass incarceration and racial disparities in the criminal justice system. By tracing the evolution of punishment from slavery through convict leasing to the modern prison system, the book reveals how institutions like Parchman Farm were designed to maintain racial hierarchies and exploit Black labor even after the formal abolition of slavery.

This historical perspective is crucial for contextualizing contemporary wrongful convictions and understanding the systemic reforms needed to address them. The book suggests that addressing wrongful convictions requires not just technical fixes to forensic procedures or legal processes, but a fundamental reckoning with the historical purpose and design of the criminal justice system itself.
