- **Key Statistics/Information**:
    - Roadside drug tests cost about $2 a piece.
    - These tests are highly unreliable and produce false positives.
    - They are used to coerce people into plea deals.
    - Substances like chocolate, cleaning supplies, aspirin, sugar, weight gain powder, candle wax, drywall, and sand can test positive on these kits.
    - ProPublica estimated about 100,000 people a year plead guilty based upon drug test kits alone.
    - In 2018, there were 1.6 million arrests for drug offenses in the US.
    - It can cost thousands of dollars to get a case expunged, even after wrongful conviction.
    - Harris County (Houston) now requires lab verification before plea deals are accepted in drug cases.

### Basic Podcast Information

- **Executive Summary**: This episode of "Wrongful Conviction: Junk Science" focuses on the unreliability of roadside drug tests and their contribution to wrongful convictions.  Hosts Kate Judson and Josh Dubin detail how these inexpensive and inaccurate tests, often misinterpreting common substances as narcotics, are used by law enforcement.  The story of Amy Albritton, wrongfully convicted based on a roadside drug test that identified a food crumb as crack cocaine, illustrates the devastating consequences.  The episode further explains how the pressure to accept plea deals, coupled with the flaws of these tests and inadequate legal representation, leads innocent individuals to plead guilty.  It highlights systemic issues within the justice system, the destruction of evidence post-plea, and the long-lasting harm to exonerees even after release. The podcast emphasizes the importance of investigative journalism in exposing these injustices and advocating for criminal justice reform.

- **Systemic Issues Identified**:
    - Use of unreliable roadside drug tests.
    - Pressure to accept plea deals due to bail, jail conditions, and fear of longer sentences.
    - Overburdened public defenders with high caseloads.
    - Backlogs in forensic labs delaying accurate testing.
    - Destruction of evidence after guilty pleas, hindering potential exoneration.
    - Difficulty in expunging records and the persistence of digital criminal records.
    - Economic and social consequences of wrongful convictions (job loss, housing insecurity, social stigma).
    - Lack of proper training for officers using drug test kits.
    - Financial incentives for companies selling cheap, unreliable drug test kits.

- **Policy Recommendations/Calls to Action**:
    - Stop using roadside drug tests as primary evidence for drug charges.
    - Require lab verification before accepting plea deals in drug cases (as implemented in Harris County).
    - Improve training for law enforcement on drug identification and testing.
    - Increase funding for forensic labs to reduce backlogs and ensure timely, accurate testing.
    - Reform plea deal practices to reduce coercion and ensure informed decisions.
    - Support investigative journalism focused on criminal justice reform and exposing junk science.
    - Advocate for easier and more affordable expungement processes, including addressing digital records.
    - Consider alternative, more reliable drug testing technologies.

- **Personal Narrative/Impact**:
    - Amy Albritton's life was "completely turned upside down" due to the wrongful conviction.
    - She lost her job, her home, and faced significant hardship.
    - The felony record made it difficult to find employment and housing even after the evidence was retested.
    - The psychological and social impact of wrongful conviction is emphasized, including the difficulty of re-assimilation into society and the lasting stigma of a criminal record.
