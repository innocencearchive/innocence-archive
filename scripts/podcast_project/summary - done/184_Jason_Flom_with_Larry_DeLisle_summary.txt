### Basic Podcast Information

-   **Executive Summary**: This episode of Wrongful Conviction with Jason Flom details the harrowing case of Larry DeLisle, wrongfully convicted for the deaths of his four children in 1989.  What was a tragic accident – his station wagon plunging into the Detroit River due to a documented accelerator defect – was twisted into murder.  Despite his wife Sue's unwavering testimony of his innocence and evidence of mechanical failure, DeLisle faced intense public scrutiny and a relentless police interrogation lasting 18 hours.  This coercive interrogation led to a questionable confession, later suppressed, but not before it poisoned public opinion.  Convicted on circumstantial evidence and facing overwhelming prejudice, DeLisle has spent over three decades in prison.  The episode highlights the systemic failures that led to his wrongful conviction and his ongoing fight for clemency.

-   **Systemic Issues Identified**:
    -   **False Confessions**: Coercive interrogation tactics leading to unreliable confessions.
    -   **Prejudicial Pre-Trial Publicity**: Media and police actions poisoning the jury pool and making a fair trial impossible in the original venue.
    -   **Confirmation Bias**: Law enforcement and public prematurely concluding guilt and overlooking exculpatory evidence (mechanical defect).
    -   **Jury Pressure**:  Potential for jurors to be pressured into verdicts for expediency rather than based on evidence.
    -   **Slow Appeals Process**:  Lengthy and often unsuccessful appeals processes for wrongful convictions.
    -   **Underutilized Clemency Power**: Clemency as a critical but underused tool to correct miscarriages of justice.
-   **Policy Recommendations/Calls to Action**:
    -   Support Larry DeLisle's clemency petition to Governor Whitmer.

    -   Advocate for reforms to prevent prejudicial pre-trial publicity.
    -   Support organizations like the Innocence Project to help exonerate the wrongfully convicted.
    -   Encourage use of clemency powers to rectify wrongful convictions.
