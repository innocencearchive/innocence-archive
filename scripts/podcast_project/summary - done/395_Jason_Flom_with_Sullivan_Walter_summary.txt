### Basic Podcast Information

-   **Executive Summary**: This episode of Wrongful Conviction features the harrowing story of Sullivan Walter, who was wrongfully convicted of rape in New Orleans in 1986 and served 36 years in prison. Despite lacking a Jerry curl and facial hair as described by the victim, and crucially, despite forensic evidence proving the perpetrator was a non-secretor while Sullivan is a secretor, he was convicted based on a questionable eyewitness identification.  The podcast details the egregious errors and systemic failures, including altered testimony by a criminalist and ineffective legal representation, that kept Sullivan imprisoned for decades after his innocence was scientifically demonstrable.  It highlights the devastating impact of wrongful conviction, the lengthy fight for exoneration, and the urgent need for systemic reform within the criminal justice system, ultimately culminating in Sullivan's release in 2022 thanks to the work of Innocence Project New Orleans.

### Wrongfully Convicted Individuals Featured

-   **Name**: Sullivan Walter
-   **Case Location**: New Orleans, Louisiana, USA
-   **Conviction Year**: 1986
-   **Exoneration Year**: 2022
-   **Crime Convicted Of**: Rape, two counts of crime against nature
-   **Sentence**: 35 years
-   **Time Served**: 36 years
-   **Contributing Factors**:
    - Eyewitness misidentification (cross-racial identification)
    - Forensic error/misinterpretation (initially, later revealed as deliberate misrepresentation of secretor status evidence)
    - Ineffective assistance of counsel (at trial and during appeals)
    - Prosecutorial misconduct (non-disclosure of exculpatory evidence, allowing false testimony)
-   **Legal Status**: Exonerated
-   **Organizations Involved**: Innocence Project New Orleans (IPNO), The Anderson Project (client services post-release)
-   **Innocence Lawyer Details**: Richard Davis, Legal Director of Innocence Project New Orleans (IPNO), played a crucial role in Sullivan's exoneration. He researched the case, identified legal avenues for review, and presented the evidence of innocence to the Civil Rights Division of the District Attorney's Office.
-   **Cross-Reference**: Not mentioned if in a specific exoneree database, but case is a clear example of wrongful conviction.
-   **Narrative of Original Crime**: On May 9, 1986, a masked, knife-wielding man broke into a woman's apartment in the Garden District of New Orleans, attacked her in the bathroom, put a t-shirt over her head, forced her to perform oral sex, and raped her twice.
-   **Key Evidence at Trial (Prosecution)**:
    - Eyewitness identification by the victim from a photo array, including Polaroids of Sullivan wearing a baseball cap.
    - Implicit suggestion that Sullivan matched a composite sketch, despite discrepancies.
-   **Trial Defender Notes**:  Defense lawyer did not present any evidence on Sullivan's behalf. The trial lasted only about an hour. The defense lawyer raised the late disclosure of the blood typing report in an initial appeal, but the brief was short and insufficient.  At a later hearing for a new trial, the defense lawyer did not challenge the criminalist's altered testimony or present independent expert testimony.
-   **Key Evidence for Exoneration/Change in Legal Status**:
    - Scientific evidence: Serology testing revealed the perpetrator was a non-secretor. Sullivan Walter is a secretor (blood type B), making it impossible for him to be the perpetrator. This was confirmed in January 1988.
    - Recantation/Inconsistency of Criminalist's Testimony: Criminalist Harry O'Neill initially testified at trial that the assailant was a non-secretor. At a later hearing for a new trial, he altered his testimony, claiming uncertainty about the secretor status, which was scientifically refuted by serologist Alan Keel.
    - New DA's Office:  Jason Williams elected as District Attorney in 2020, ushering in a more reform-minded era, and the Civil Rights Division under Melissa Montel recognized the injustice in Sullivan's case.
-   **Actual Perpetrator Details**: Not mentioned in the transcript. The actual perpetrator of the rape was never identified or brought to justice.

-   **Systemic Issues Identified**:
    - Flawed eyewitness identification procedures, especially cross-racial identifications.
    - Misuse and misrepresentation of forensic evidence, including potential perjury by forensic analysts.
    - Ineffective assistance of counsel at trial and during appeals.
    - Prosecutorial misconduct, including Brady violations (non-disclosure of exculpatory evidence).
    - Judicial inaction and resistance to correcting wrongful convictions, even with clear evidence of innocence.
    - The devastating impact of "tough on crime" eras and the influence of figures like DA Harry Connick Sr. on creating a system prone to wrongful convictions.
    - The lengthy and arduous process for exoneration, even with compelling evidence of innocence.
-   **Policy Recommendations/Calls to Action**:
    - Support for organizations like Innocence Project New Orleans (IPNO).
    - Reforming eyewitness identification procedures to minimize suggestiveness and cross-racial misidentification.
    - Ensuring proper handling and interpretation of forensic evidence, and accountability for forensic analysts.
    - Improving access to effective legal counsel for indigent defendants, including post-conviction.
    - Strengthening Brady rules and ensuring accountability for prosecutorial misconduct.
    - Creating mechanisms for quicker and more effective review of potential wrongful conviction cases.
-   **Personal Narrative/Impact**:
    - Sullivan Walter describes the profound personal impact of wrongful conviction, including the trauma of prison as a sex offender, the loss of family relationships (mother, father, sister), and the loss of his youth and life opportunities. He emphasizes the psychological toll and his struggle to survive and maintain hope despite decades of injustice. He also highlights the victim's secondary victimization by the failure to find the actual perpetrator and bring them to justice.
