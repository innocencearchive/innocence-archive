### Basic Podcast Information

-   **Executive Summary**: This episode of Wrongful Conviction: Junk Science, hosted by Kate Judson and Josh Dubin, delves into the dangers of eyewitness testimony and its role in wrongful convictions. The episode highlights the story of Marvin Anderson, who was wrongfully convicted of rape based on misidentification and served 15 years in prison before DNA evidence exonerated him.  Psychologist Elizabeth Loftus is interviewed, explaining how memory is malleable and can be easily influenced by misinformation and suggestive questioning. The episode discusses experiments by Hugo Munsterberg and Loftus demonstrating the fallibility of memory.  It emphasizes that eyewitness testimony, despite being convincing to juries, is often unreliable and a major contributing factor in wrongful convictions.  The hosts discuss reforms needed in police procedures, such as double-blind lineups and unbiased instructions to witnesses, to improve the accuracy of eyewitness identifications and prevent future wrongful convictions. The episode concludes with a call to action to consider the circumstances surrounding eyewitness testimony and remain open to the possibility of memory error to prevent wrongful convictions.

-   **Systemic Issues Identified**:
    -   Unreliability of eyewitness testimony.
    -   Suggestive police procedures in lineups (photo and live).
    -   Confirmation bias from law enforcement.
    -   Juries over-reliance on eyewitness accounts.
    -   Lack of understanding of memory malleability in the legal system.
-   **Policy Recommendations/Calls to Action**:
    -   Implement double-blind lineups.
    -   Provide unbiased instructions to eyewitnesses during lineups.
    -   Use fillers in lineups that match the witness's description of the perpetrator, not just resemble the suspect.
    -   Educate law enforcement and the public about the fallibility of memory and eyewitness testimony.
    -   Consider surrounding circumstances and potential influences on eyewitness memory in legal cases.
